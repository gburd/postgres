\title{Bounded Clock Sweep: Eviction That Cannot Be Starved}

\author{Greg Burd}
\affiliation{\institution{}\city{}\country{}}
\email{greg@burd.me}

\begin{abstract}
PostgreSQL chooses eviction victims with a CLOCK sweep over a four-bit
\texttt{usage\_count} saturating at five. A buffer is evictable only when that
counter reaches zero, and every access resets it to five. The consequence is
not a slow average: it is the absence of any bound. We show that the worst-case
work of a single buffer allocation equals the entire buffer pool, measured to
five significant figures at pool sizes from 4\,GiB to 96\,GiB, on both
two-socket and six-node NUMA hardware. One buffer read sweeps a million
descriptors, and the cost grows linearly with \texttt{shared\_buffers}.

We give the mechanism as a residency argument: with pass time $T$ and
per-page access rate $r$, the fraction of the pool that is evictable decays as
$e^{-krT}$ for a counter of depth $k$. Reducing $k$ from five to one---a
one-bit cooling state in the manner of LeanStore---improves the constant by
$1.75\times$ but leaves the exponent, and we measure the worst case still at
$0.998$ of the pool. Fewer states is a better constant, not a bound.

The bound requires abandoning probation when the evidence says it is
unaffordable. A sweep that has demoted a threshold number of buffers without
finding one evictable has established that the pool is hotter than the hand can
traverse; it then claims the next unpinned buffer outright. Worst-case
allocation work becomes $33$--$37$ clock advances, constant in pool size: $O(1)$
in place of $O(N_{\text{buffers}})$, a reduction of $3.7\times10^5$ at
96\,GiB. Throughput and hit ratio are unchanged. We argue this is the correct
shape for the result: the pathology was never average cost, and a fix that
moved average cost would be solving a different problem.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{buffer management, page replacement, CLOCK, tail latency, PostgreSQL}

\section{Introduction}

A buffer pool manager must answer one question repeatedly and quickly: which
page do I throw away? PostgreSQL has answered it the same way for two decades,
with a CLOCK sweep over a saturating counter, and the answer has been good
enough that the code has needed little attention. This paper is about a
condition under which it stops being good enough, and about why the condition
is invisible to the benchmarks one would normally reach for.

The mechanism is simple enough to state in a sentence. A buffer is evictable
only once its \texttt{usage\_count} has been decremented to zero; each access
restores the counter to its maximum; therefore a page must escape the clock
hand's attention for five consecutive passes to become a victim. Whether that
is achievable depends on a race between the hand and the workload, and the
hand gets slower as the pool gets larger. When the workload wins, the supply of
victims collapses---and because decrementing a counter is accounted as progress
by the search loop, the search has no reason to stop. One page read can sweep
the entire pool.

We find this is not a corner case requiring adversarial construction. It needs
only a pool large enough that a pass takes real time, a working set slightly
larger than the pool, and skew. All three are ordinary properties of a large
production database. What it does \emph{not} need---and this is why it has been
hard to see---is high throughput, contention, or I/O pressure. In our
measurements throughput is flat while the worst-case allocation cost varies by
five orders of magnitude.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A residency model (\S\ref{sec:model}) predicting that eviction-candidate
density decays as $e^{-krT}$, and therefore that counter depth $k$ sets a
constant while pool size sets the exponent.
\item Measurement (\S\ref{sec:unbounded}) that PostgreSQL's worst-case
allocation equals $N_{\text{buffers}}$ to five significant figures across a
$24\times$ range of pool sizes, on two NUMA topologies.
\item The negative result (\S\ref{sec:onebit}) that a one-bit cooling state,
which we had previously proposed on grounds of simplicity, does \emph{not} fix
this: worst case remains $0.998$ of the pool.
\item A bound (\S\ref{sec:claim}) that is evidence-driven rather than
configured, reducing worst-case work to $O(1)$ while leaving the common path
untouched, and a background variant (\S\ref{sec:precool}) that moves the
remaining work off the allocating backend.
\item An honest accounting (\S\ref{sec:nothing}) of what does not change:
throughput, hit ratio, and the need for the existing ring buffers.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}

\subsection{CLOCK and its descendants}

CLOCK~\cite{corbato1968paging} approximates LRU with one reference bit per
frame and a rotating hand, trading precision for the absence of a lock on every
hit. The approximation has been refined many times: WSCLOCK~\cite{carr1981wsclock}
adds a working-set criterion, LRU-K~\cite{oneil1993lruk} and
2Q~\cite{johnson1994lru2} separate recency from frequency,
LIRS~\cite{jiang2002lirs} and CLOCK-Pro~\cite{jiang2005clockpro} reason about
reuse distance, ARC~\cite{megiddo2003arc} adapts the recency/frequency balance
online, and S3-FIFO~\cite{yang2023s3fifo} argues much of this can be recovered
with queues alone. LeanStore~\cite{leanstore2018} introduces a \emph{cooling
stage}: a page is hot or cooling, and cooling pages form the eviction pool.

Almost all of this literature optimises hit ratio. The question we ask is
different and much less studied: not how good the victim is, but how long it
takes to find one, and whether that time is bounded. Gray and
Reuter~\cite{gray1992transaction} treat buffer search cost as a constant.
Database-specific buffer management in the DBMIN
tradition~\cite{chou1985dbmin,stonebraker1981os} is concerned with allocating
the pool between queries, not with the cost of a single allocation.

\subsection{PostgreSQL's sweep}

PostgreSQL stores a four-bit \texttt{usage\_count} in the packed 64-bit buffer
state word, saturating at $\texttt{BM\_MAX\_USAGE\_COUNT}=5$. The relevant code
is \texttt{StrategyGetBuffer()}~\cite{postgresql_freelist}:

\begin{lstlisting}
trycounter = NBuffers;
for (;;) {
  buf = GetBufferDescriptor(ClockSweepTick());
  if (BUF_STATE_GET_REFCOUNT(state) != 0) {
    if (--trycounter == 0)
      elog(ERROR, "no unpinned buffers available");
    continue;
  }
  if (BUF_STATE_GET_USAGECOUNT(state) != 0) {
    state -= BUF_USAGECOUNT_ONE;   /* decrement */
    if (CAS(&buf->state, state))
      trycounter = NBuffers;       /* progress! */
    continue;
  }
  /* usage_count == 0: claim it */
  return buf;
}
\end{lstlisting}

Two properties of this loop matter. First, a decrement \emph{resets}
\texttt{trycounter}: cooling is accounted as progress, so the only way to
terminate without a victim is to find every buffer pinned. Second, the source
itself documents the consequence, in a comment that has been correct and
unremarked for years: ``it can take as many as
\texttt{BM\_MAX\_USAGE\_COUNT+1} complete cycles of the clock-sweep hand to
find a free buffer.'' Six passes of a twelve-million-buffer pool is the
documented worst case. Our contribution is to observe that this is not a
pessimistic bound reached only in theory, but the \emph{measured} behaviour
under ordinary conditions.

\section{A residency model}
\label{sec:model}

Let $T$ be the wall-clock time for the hand to complete one pass, and model
accesses to a given page as Poisson with rate $r$. For a counter of depth $k$
that saturates on access, a page becomes evictable only if it survives $k$
consecutive passes untouched, which requires an access-free interval of length
$kT$. The probability of that is $e^{-krT}$, so the steady-state fraction of
the pool that is evictable---the \emph{candidate density} $\rho$---is

\begin{equation}
\rho(k, r, T) \;\approx\; e^{-k r T}.
\label{eq:density}
\end{equation}

The expected number of clock advances per victim is then $1/\rho = e^{krT}$:
amplification is \emph{exponential} in the product of counter depth, access
rate, and pass time. Since $T \propto N_{\text{buffers}}$ for a hand that
advances at a fixed rate, growing the pool grows the exponent.

Figure~\ref{fig:density} plots Equation~\ref{eq:density} for $k=5$ and $k=1$.
Two readings are worth drawing out, because they predict the empirical results
and they are not symmetric.

First, the shift is real and large. At $rT=1$, $k=5$ leaves $0.67\%$ of the
pool evictable while $k=1$ leaves $37\%$---a factor of $55$. A workload that
starves the stock sweep may be comfortable for a one-bit one.

Second, and more importantly, the shift is only a shift. Both curves go to
zero. For any $k$ there is an $rT$ beyond which density is negligible, and
since $T$ grows with the pool, a sufficiently large pool reaches that point for
any fixed workload. \emph{No choice of counter depth yields a bound.} This is
the theoretical statement of which \S\ref{sec:onebit} is the measurement, and
it is why the fix in \S\ref{sec:claim} has to change the loop's termination
rule rather than its state encoding.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Hardware.} Two bare-metal cloud instances: \textbf{z1d.metal}
(48 cores, 2 NUMA nodes, 384\,GiB, data directory on a RAID-0 of local NVMe)
and \textbf{r8i.metal-96xl} (384 cores, 6 NUMA nodes via sub-NUMA clustering,
3\,TiB). Both with huge pages enabled, transparent huge pages disabled, NUMA
balancing off.

\paragraph{Builds.} Four variants of PostgreSQL 20devel, compiled
\texttt{debugoptimized} with assertions off, differing \emph{only} in the
eviction path: \textsc{master} (0..5 counter), \textsc{1-bit} (cooling state),
\textsc{+claim} (\S\ref{sec:claim}), \textsc{+precool} (\S\ref{sec:precool}).
All four carry an identical instrument recording clock advances, victims
produced, demotions, the maximum advances consumed by any single allocation,
and claims.

\paragraph{Workload.} This deserves emphasis, because it is where we went wrong
for several months. The regime requires:
\begin{itemize}
\item a pool large enough that a pass takes real time;
\item a working set only \emph{slightly} larger than the pool ($1.15\times$);
\item \emph{skewed} access (\texttt{random\_zipfian}, $s=1.10$);
\item enough concurrency that re-promotion races the hand.
\end{itemize}
Our earlier benchmarks used uniform access over a working set several times the
pool. That is the natural choice for exercising eviction, and it cannot
reproduce this pathology at all: under uniform access each individual page has
a vanishing access rate, so $rT \approx 0$, nothing is ever re-promoted before
the hand returns, and candidate density stays near one. Uniform access measures
eviction \emph{throughput}; it is blind to eviction \emph{starvation}. We
report this because it is the single most useful methodological finding here,
and because a reader trying to reproduce the result will otherwise reach for the
same wrong workload.

Three runs per point, counters sampled after a 60\,s warmup and differenced.

The result is independent of NUMA topology. \textsc{master} pins to the
\emph{same} maximum, $1{,}048{,}614$, on two nodes and on six, though

the pathology is a property of the replacement algorithm, not of memory
placement or interconnect behaviour, and it will not be fixed by the
NUMA-partitioning work currently in progress~\cite{vondra_numa}---partitioning
divides the pool, which reduces $T$ per partition, but leaves the exponential
intact within each.

\section{Fewer states is not a bound}
\label{sec:onebit}

We had previously proposed replacing \texttt{usage\_count} with a one-bit
cooling state~\cite{leanstore2018}: a buffer is \textsc{hot} or \textsc{cool},
a demand-loaded page is admitted \textsc{cool}, a second access promotes it,
and the sweep demotes \textsc{hot} buffers and reclaims \textsc{cool} ones. The
argument was simplicity---two states and no magic constant in place of six
states and a tunable five---and the measured effect on throughput was nil.

Table~\ref{tab:main} shows what that change actually does. Amplification
improves consistently: $5.90 \rightarrow 3.36$ ticks per victim at 8\,GiB on
two nodes, $6.01 \rightarrow 3.41$ on six, a factor of about $1.75$. This is
Equation~\ref{eq:density} behaving as predicted: $k$ falls from five to one and
the constant improves.

And the worst case does not move. It is $0.9981$ of the pool at 8\,GiB and
$0.9995$ at 32\,GiB---still a full pass, still $O(N_{\text{buffers}})$. A page
that is re-promoted within $T$ is immortal whether promotion sets a counter to
five or a bit to one. The one-bit evictor buys a $5\times$ margin in $rT$ and
nothing in asymptotics.

We state this plainly because we got it wrong in the other direction first,
arguing for the state change on aesthetic grounds while the measurable
pathology went unaddressed. The state encoding was never the problem. The
termination rule is.

\section{A bound from evidence}
\label{sec:claim}

The loop's defect is that it treats cooling as progress without limit. It will
demote forever, because each demotion is locally a step toward a victim even
when globally the workload is restoring them faster.

The fix is to notice when that is happening and stop insisting on probation.
Probation exists so that a buffer gets one sweep of grace in which a new access
can rescue it; that is a good default, but it is affordable only while victims
can still be found. Once a single call has demoted $\Theta$ buffers without
encountering one evictable, the pool is \emph{demonstrably} hotter than the
hand can traverse---this is not an estimate or a heuristic threshold on some
proxy, it is a direct observation of the quantity that matters---and the sweep
claims the next unpinned buffer outright:

\begin{lstlisting}
if (BUF_STATE_GET_COOLSTATE(state) != COOL) {
  if (cooled < BUF_COOL_CLAIM_THRESHOLD) {
    state &= ~BUF_USAGECOUNT_MASK;   /* HOT -> COOL */
    if (CAS(&buf->state, state)) {
      cooled++;
      trycounter = NBuffers;
      break;
    }
    continue;
  }
  /* pressure: claim it. The cooling state is cleared
   * when the victim is reused, so no demotion first. */
  pg_atomic_fetch_add_u64(&ctl->numCoolClaims, 1);
}
state += BUF_REFCOUNT_ONE;           /* claim */
\end{lstlisting}

Three properties make this cheap. The counter \texttt{cooled} is
call-local---no shared state, no atomic on the common path. The threshold is
derived from the cache line size rather than exposed as a GUC, so it adds no
tuning surface. And under any workload the hand can keep up with, the counter
never reaches the threshold and the code path is \emph{identical} to before;
this is a change to behaviour under starvation only.

The measured effect is in Table~\ref{tab:main}: worst case $33$--$37$ advances
at every pool size and topology. Constant, not merely smaller. At 96\,GiB that
is a reduction of $3.7\times 10^5$. Amplification is unchanged
($3.39$ vs.\ $3.36$ at 8\,GiB), which is the expected and desirable outcome:
the claim path fires only in the tail, so it should not perturb the mean.

The cost is honest and worth naming: a claimed buffer is evicted without having
served its probation, so in the starved regime the algorithm's replacement
quality degrades toward FIFO. We consider this the right trade, because the
alternative in that regime is an unbounded scan, and because a cache that
cannot find a victim has already lost. The shared \texttt{numCoolClaims}
counter records how often this occurs, which is a useful operational signal in
its own right: a rising value means the pool is undersized for its access rate.

\section{Moving the work off the critical path}
\label{sec:precool}

The claim bound leaves the search on the allocating backend. The background
writer's cleaning scan is already walking buffers ahead of the hand and already
holds each buffer header lock, so demoting an unpinned \textsc{hot} buffer as
it passes costs one masked store on work being performed anyway, with no CAS
and no additional lock acquisition.

The design question is when to do it. A fixed background cooler would shorten
every buffer's probation unconditionally, trading hit ratio for a problem that
may not exist---the mistake available here. Instead the scan consults
\texttt{numCoolClaims} and stages candidates only while that count is
\emph{rising}, i.e.\ only while the foreground is demonstrably starving. Staged
buffers count toward the writer's existing \texttt{reusable\_buffers} budget,
so the demand estimate that already bounds cleaning also bounds staging.

This yields a further $9\%$ reduction in amplification ($3.39 \rightarrow 3.09$
at 8\,GiB on two nodes; $3.42 \rightarrow 3.05$ on six) while preserving full
probation whenever nothing is waiting. The worst case is unchanged at $34$--$35$,
as it must be---the bound is the claim path's doing, not the writer's.

\section{What does not change}
\label{sec:nothing}

\paragraph{Throughput and hit ratio.} Flat. Across all four arms at every point
measured, throughput agrees within $2\%$ with overlapping run-to-run ranges
($613$k vs.\ $614$k transactions/s on two nodes; $967$k vs.\ $988$k on six),
and buffer hit ratio within $0.1$ percentage points. We regard this as
confirmation rather than disappointment. The pathology is a tail phenomenon; a
change that moved the mean would be evidence of having altered something other
than the thing under study.

\paragraph{The ring buffers.} Admitting pages \textsc{cool} makes a read-once
page self-evicting, which is the pollution-avoidance role PostgreSQL's
\texttt{BufferAccessStrategy} rings were introduced to play, so it is fair to
ask whether they remain necessary. They do, for reasons the evictor does not
reach: \texttt{BAS\_BULKREAD} drives a rejection callback that declines victims
whose eviction would force a WAL flush, and the write strategies dirty their
buffers on first touch, so admission policy decides which buffer is reused but
not whether reuse entails a write.

We did test the vacuum ring, because PostgreSQL already permits disabling it at
runtime and so the experiment needs no patch. With this series applied,
disabling it left concurrent OLTP throughput and $p99$ latency unchanged within
run-to-run spread while making \texttt{VACUUM} itself $3.3\times$ faster
($66.6$\,s $\rightarrow$ $19.9$\,s, non-overlapping). That is evidence
\emph{for} removability and against the writeback-pacing justification usually
offered---including the one we offered ourselves before measuring. It is not
sufficient to act on: one workload shape, one storage class, explicit
\texttt{VACUUM} rather than concurrent autovacuum workers, and removal would
also cost a documented I/O-accounting context and a user-facing tuning knob. We
report the measurement and leave the ring in place.

\section{Threats to validity}

Our evidence that this pathology costs production users is second-hand: reports
of multi-second stalls on large instances, consistent with the mechanism but
not instrumented by us. The cheapest confirmation available to any operator
requires no patch---if \texttt{pg\_buffercache\_usage\_counts()} shows the pool
piled at \texttt{usage\_count}~5 with little at~0, the sweep has no candidates
and every allocation is paying for the search.

The threshold $\Theta$ is cache-line-derived and we have not characterised its
sensitivity; whether it should adapt to observed candidate density is open. We
have not constructed a workload in which claim-induced premature eviction
measurably harms hit ratio, which is a gap in the argument rather than evidence
of its absence. Our pool sizes reach 96\,GiB where production instances reach
terabytes, and $T$ grows with the pool, so we expect the effect we report to be
a lower bound. Finally, all measurements are single-instance; replication and
recovery workloads are unexamined.

\section{Conclusion}

PostgreSQL's clock sweep has an unbounded worst case, and the bound is not
missing by a little: a single buffer allocation can sweep the entire pool, and
does, reproducibly, at every pool size we measured and on both NUMA topologies
we tried. The source code has documented this possibility for years without it
being recognised as a thing that actually happens.

The appealing fix---fewer states, a cleaner encoding, one bit instead of
six---improves the constant by $1.75\times$ and leaves the asymptotics
untouched. We know because we proposed it, measured it, and found the worst case
still at $0.998$ of the pool. Bounding the work requires a different kind of
change: allowing the sweep to conclude, from direct observation rather than
configuration, that probation has become unaffordable, and to stop. That makes
the worst case constant in pool size at a cost of $33$--$37$ clock advances,
perturbs nothing on the common path, and leaves throughput and hit ratio where
they were.

The broader lesson concerns the choice of workload. Uniform access over a
working set much larger than the buffer pool is the obvious way to exercise
eviction, and it is precisely blind to eviction starvation, because it gives no
individual page an access rate high enough to outrun the clock. The pathology
lives where the working set is barely larger than the pool and the access
distribution is skewed---which is to say, where production databases live.
